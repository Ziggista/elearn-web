About 20 to 25 minutes. One story the whole way through: Chris wants to talk to Sally securely. The written version is the "TLS and trust" page on this site.

The cast

Meet everyone

🖥️

Sally

The server. app.rrc.qld.gov.au on IIS.

🧑‍💻

Chris

The client. A browser, an app, or another server.

🏛️

Charlie

The Certificate Authority. Vouches for people.

👂

Eve

Eavesdropper. Can see the traffic.

😈

Mallory

Attacker. Can change traffic and pretend.

Sally and Chris are the good guys. Charlie is the passport office. Eve and Mallory are the reason we need TLS. Eve could be anyone on shared Wi-Fi or a compromised switch; Mallory is someone who can sit in the middle of the connection.

Why we need TLS

Without TLS, every message is a postcard

🧑‍💻
Chris
📮 user=chris
password=Summer2026!
👂 Eve reads it😈 Mallory changes it
🖥️
Sally?

👂 Read

Passwords, payroll, query results in plain text.

✏️ Change

Swap a bank account number, inject a script.

🎭 Pretend

Answer as “Sally”. Chris can’t tell.

Every router, switch, access point and ISP along the path can read a postcard. Ask: how many network devices sit between your desk and the SQL server? Each one is a place Eve could be.

What TLS is

A sealed envelope, delivered to a checked address

✉️

Confidentiality

Opaque envelope. Eve sees noise.

🧷

Integrity

Tamper seal. Any change is detected.

🪪

Authentication

The address was checked against ID.

Encrypting a chat with Mallory protects nothing.

Authentication is what makes the encryption worth having.

This is the key idea of the session. People think TLS = encryption. But a perfectly encrypted connection to the wrong server is useless. The rest of the deck is about how Chris knows he's really talking to Sally.

The trust problem

“Hi, I’m Sally.” Anyone can say that.

🖥️🔑

Real Sally

Has a key pair. Hands out her public key, keeps her private key on the server.

😈🔑

Mallory

Generates her own key pair in 2 seconds and also says “I’m Sally, here’s my public key.”

Chris has never met Sally. He needs someone he already trusts to vouch for her.

Public keys are just numbers. Nothing about a public key says who it belongs to. That's the gap certificates fill.

Enter Charlie

Charlie the CA is a passport office

1
Sally applies

Sends her public key and the name she wants (a CSR). Keeps the private key.

2
Charlie checks

Sally proves she controls app.rrc.qld.gov.au (DNS record or web file). Mallory can’t.

3
Charlie signs

“This public key belongs to app.rrc.qld.gov.au until 17 Sep.” Signed with Charlie’s private key.

🪪

That signed statement is Sally’s certificate: an ID card for her public key.

Our internal CA does the same job for corp.local names, except the "proof" is that the request comes from a domain-joined machine or an admin.

Why Chris trusts Charlie

Charlie was on Chris’s computer first

🧑‍💻

Chris trusts

Charlie’s root, already in his trust store

→
🏛️

Charlie vouches

Sally’s public key is app.rrc.qld.gov.au

→
🖥️

Sally proves

She holds the matching private key

Windows, macOS, browsers and Java ship with a few hundred audited roots. Group Policy adds our internal CA to domain PCs. Break any link and Chris walks away.

This is the whole of "trust" in one slide. In practice there's an intermediate between Charlie's root and Sally, and Sally has to send it. Same idea, one more link.
😈 Mallory: Hi Chris, it’s me, Sally 👋
🧑‍💻 Chris: Cool. ID please.
😈 Mallory: Here’s my certificate. I signed it myself.
🧑‍💻 Chris: Sir, this is a self-signed certificate. 🚪
A self-signed certificate is someone vouching for themselves. That's exactly what Chris can't accept from a stranger.

The handshake

How Chris and Sally start talking securely

🧑‍💻Chris
🖥️Sally

1 · Hello. I want app.rrc.qld.gov.au, I speak TLS 1.3. Here’s my half of the key exchange.

2 · Hello. My half of the key exchange, my certificate + intermediate, and a signature with my private key.

3 · Chris checks name, dates, chain, revocation, and Sally’s signature. ✅

4 · Both compute the same shared secret. It is never sent.

5 · 🔒 Encrypted conversation with fast symmetric keys.

Five steps, one round trip in TLS 1.3. The next few slides zoom in on steps 2, 3 and 4.

Step 2 · Sally proves it’s her

A certificate can be copied. A signature can’t.

📄

The certificate is public

Anyone, including Mallory, can download Sally’s certificate and send it.

✍️

The signature is the proof

Sally signs this exact handshake with her private key. Only the real Sally can.

Mallory with a copied cert gets stuck right here. That’s why the private key is the crown jewel. 👑

If the private key leaks, Mallory can be Sally. Remember this for the save now, decrypt later section at the end.

Step 3 · Chris checks

Four checks, any fail = stop

🏷️

Name

Is app.rrc.qld.gov.au in the SAN?

📅

Dates

Is it valid today?

⛓️

Chain

Does it lead to Charlie in my trust store?

🚫

Revocation

Has Charlie cancelled it?

Then Chris verifies Sally’s signature with the public key from the cert. Pass = “I’m talking to the holder of Sally’s private key, and Charlie says that’s app.rrc.qld.gov.au.”

Every certificate error your users see is one of these four failing. Clicking through a warning, curl -k or TrustServerCertificate=True skips them, which hands the connection to Mallory.

Step 4 · A secret that’s never sent

Key exchange, explained with paint

🧑‍💻 Chris

Shared: yellow

Secret: red

Sends: yellow+red

👂 Eve sees
yellow, orange, green

Can’t un-mix paint

🖥️ Sally

Shared: yellow

Secret: blue

Sends: yellow+blue

Each adds their own secret to what they received. Both get yellow + red + blue. Same colour, never transmitted.

This is ECDHE, Elliptic-Curve Diffie-Hellman Ephemeral. "Ephemeral" means the secret colours are picked fresh for every connection and thrown away afterwards. Hold onto that word for the last section.

What if Mallory tries?

Every trick hits a check

😈 Mallory’s moveWhat stops her
Answers with her own self-signed cert⛓️ Chain check
Uses a real cert for a name she owns🏷️ Name check
Copies Sally’s real certificate✍️ Can’t make Sally’s signature
Changes traffic after the handshake🧷 Integrity check, connection dropped
Records everything for later🤔 Encrypted noise… for now
The last row is the cliffhanger. Recording is cheap and passive. What happens if Mallory gets something later that she doesn't have today?

The long game

Save now, decrypt later

📼

Today

Record encrypted traffic. Cheap, silent, undetectable.

⏳

Wait

For a stolen key, or for the maths to break.

🔓

Later

Decrypt what was captured years ago.

Payroll, HR, legal, ratepayer and SCADA data stay sensitive for years. A breach in 2032 of data sent in 2026 is still a breach.

Also called harvest now, decrypt later. Two ways "later" arrives: the server's private key leaks (Heartbleed), or the public-key maths gets broken (quantum).

Later, version 1 · The key leaks

Heartbleed (2014): servers bled their private keys

💔 What happened

Bug in OpenSSL (CVE-2014-0160). A crafted heartbeat made servers return 64 KB of memory per request, no logs. Researchers pulled out private TLS keys within days.

📼 Why recordings mattered

With old RSA key exchange, the session secret was encrypted with Sally’s public key. Steal her private key later and every recorded session opens up.

Key exchangeSally’s key stolen later
TLS_RSA_… (old TLS 1.2)❌ All recorded past traffic readable
ECDHE (TLS 1.3 / modern 1.2)✅ Past sessions safe: forward secrecy
The paint colours in ECDHE are ephemeral: picked fresh, thrown away. Stealing Sally's long-term key later doesn't recover them. Heartbleed is why forward secrecy became mandatory, and why TLS 1.3 removed RSA key exchange. Other lessons: patching wasn't enough, you had to replace the key, reissue and revoke. And OpenSSL was hiding inside appliances and vendor software. Windows SChannel wasn't affected, but Apache, PHP and appliances next to it were.

Later, version 2 · The maths breaks

Quantum: Heartbleed for everyone, at once

⚛️

The threat

A large quantum computer is expected to break RSA and ECDH. Forward secrecy doesn’t help if the key exchange itself falls. Recordings made today are the target.

🛡️

The response

ML-KEM (NIST FIPS 203) is quantum-resistant. Browsers and big providers already use a hybrid (X25519MLKEM768): safe if either half holds.

ASD guidance for Australian government: plan to stop relying on traditional asymmetric crypto by 2030.

Nobody has the machine yet. That's the point of save now, decrypt later: they don't need it yet. Data sent today that must stay secret past ~2030 is already at risk if it's only protected by classic key exchange.

What we do about it

Five things for our servers

1

TLS 1.3 where possible. On TLS 1.2 allow only ECDHE suites; turn off TLS_RSA_….

2

Patch TLS libraries, including OpenSSL, Java and .NET bundled in appliances and vendor apps.

3

Key might have leaked? Replace it. New key, new cert, revoke the old. Never just patch.

4

Know where every cert and key lives, so the next Heartbleed is a list, not a hunt.

5

Ask vendors about post-quantum (hybrid ML-KEM) support and roadmaps.

Number 4 is where the CMDB work comes in: certificates and the software that terminates TLS should be in the inventory.

Recap

Chris trusts Charlie.
Charlie vouches for Sally.
Sally proves she holds the key.
The secret is never sent.
